The prompts I used

Edited for spelling and clarity. The requests are the same as the ones I sent.

The game itself: two tries.

First prompt.

My professor wants a game for the lab sprint. Let’s turn it into a real video game.

Why it fell short: it didn’t say who the game was for, how long students had, how they turn in their work, or what “video game” should mean. The AI had only the existing worksheet to go on, so it started from that.

Second prompt, rewritten to be direct and complete.

My professor asked me to build the Unit 5 AI Lab Sprint. She builds each sprint from the Unit Guide or the Unit Project, and students should finish it in 30 to 40 minutes. Use her Unit 3 sprint as the model for how students turn in their work. Then make it look and feel like an actual video game: players pick a character, level up as they learn, and get stronger as they go.

The AI’s response, summarized:

  • Kept the sprint’s three levels (Read it, Break it, Decide it) and the Unit 3 turn-in: a PDF of every answer, a Comprehend screenshot, and the AI Use Disclosure Form.
  • Added four characters to choose from (Triage Nurse, Data Analyst, Pharmacist, Red Teamer), each with its own stats: Insight, Skepticism, and Judgment.
  • Turned the sprint into a walkable pixel-art clinic with three rooms, XP, six levels, and a stat point to spend at every level-up.
  • Made “get stronger” mean something: Judgment sets how hard you hit the final boss, and confident wrong answers cost HP, so learning early makes the boss easier.
  • Kept the same point rules as a worksheet, so scores still compare across the class.

What I learned: the second prompt worked because it named the audience, the time limit, a model to follow, and the exact features I wanted. A vague prompt gets a guess. A clear one gets the game you pictured.

Voices in the RPG.

Give each character its own voice that reads the game’s text aloud, so players can listen instead of reading every screen.

The AI gave each character its own browser voice: Dana reads the instructions, patients read their messages, the Machine reads the AI’s output, and the boss and each class have their own voices.

The quiz show and its voices.

Build a quiz show game for two teams, where each team can type in its own name. Use one voice to read each question and a different voice to read the correct answer.

My first version of this request named two famous TV personalities for the voices. The AI would not imitate real people’s voices, so the game uses two original voices instead: a narrator for the questions and a host for the answers.

Why this matters for Unit 5

Unit 5 is about reading what AI hands you and deciding who checks it before anyone acts. The same rule applied here. The AI produced the code and the text and tested it, and then I reviewed and tested it myself before it went to students. I didn't publish anything on the AI's word alone.
